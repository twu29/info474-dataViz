Introduction
The first modern Olympic Games in 1896 only featured 14 nations, taking place in Athens to reimagine the ancient sporting tradition. Participation expanded and attendance was shaped by decolonization, political tensions, and new independence. Today, there are over 200 nations competing in the Olympics, which has reshaped the medaling landscape and dominance.
Which countries have dominated the Summer Olympics, and how have medal counts shifted over time?
The United States has been the most dominant force in the Summer Olympics, consistently earning the largest medal counts from the early 1900s through 2020. Russia emerged as a major competitor during the Cold War era, with its largest medal hauls appearing between 1960 and 1980, before declining after the fall of the Soviet Union. Germany shows notable gaps during the World Wars but remained a strong contender throughout the 20th century.

Meanwhile, countries like China and South Korea entered the scene later, growing rapidly from the 1980s onward. Traditional European powers such as Great Britain, France, Hungary, and Sweden were early participants with steady but smaller medal counts over time. The overall pattern reveals a shift from a few Western nations dominating the podium to a broader, more globally distributed medal landscape.
How about the Winter Games?
Unlike the Summer Games, the Winter Olympics have been dominated by Northern European and cold-climate nations. Russia leads with the largest medal counts, particularly from the 1960s onward during the Soviet era, maintaining its dominance into the modern games. Germany and Canada follow closely, both showing consistent and growing medal performances across nearly every Winter Olympics.

Norway, Finland, and Sweden have been steady contenders since the earliest Winter Games in 1924, reflecting their deep traditions in winter sports. Austria and Switzerland also maintain strong presences throughout. Countries like South Korea, China, and Japan entered later but have grown in recent decades, while the United States, though a top performer in the Summer Games, ranks slightly lower here compared to the Nordic powerhouses.
Age distributions of gold medalists at Olympic Games
The youngest athlete to win a gold medal achieved this feat at 13 years old. The age of medaling reveals the peaks of athletic performance across different sports and eras of the Olympics where the most optimal training regimines and experiences have evolved. Exploring the age distribution of medalists uncovers patterns of how age and achievement intersect.

In an average distribution of medalists across age ranges in the Summer Olympics, we can see that athletes aged 22-24 are most likely to win medals. This age range also highlights the most gold medalists, with a significant drop-off in medal counts as athletes age, especially in ages over 30. Ages 19-30 have the highest concentration of medalists, suggesting that this is the most successful range for Olympic performance.
Peak Performance at Winter Games
The Winter Olympic games shows a slightly different age distribution of medalists, with a peak in medal counts for athletes aged 25-27. This suggests that the age of peak performance for Winter Games is slightly older. Athletes in the range 22-30 have the highest concentration of medalists, with a steeper drop off towards upward ages from 30.

Instead of the traditional peak incline and decline pattern that we see in the Summer Games, the Winter Games has a higher average count at ages 16-18, before dropping off and then peaking at 25-28. This can suggest a more competitive landscape for young athletes around 19-21, while also supporting a longer peak performance window for athletes into their 20s.

Since 1984, the United States has consistently sent the largest delegation to the Summer Olympics, often followed by countries like Japan, Australia, and China. Before this period, the largest teams varied more from year to year, with countries such as France, Australia, Japan, and the United States alternating among the top. Overall, there is a clear trend of increasing total participation over time. While this appears as more athletes in total, smaller countries with fewer athletes are grouped together in the visualization when they do not reach the square threshold, meaning the chart may show fewer unique countries even as the total athlete count increases.
